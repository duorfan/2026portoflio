Ver Coaching / VR product design

A clearer first session in VR.

In Ver’s vision training drills, athletes were missing the cues that told them what to do. I helped pinpoint where they got stuck and prototyped ways to guide them through the drill and show progress afterward.

01 / Research

One drill kept interrupting the first session.

Athletes rated the experience well, even though facilitators sometimes had to step in. We compared survey responses with recordings and grouped pauses and misunderstandings by task to decide what to redesign first.

Targeting Arches

Finding the first signal

Some athletes missed the blinking arch or didn’t realize they needed to turn their head. Many could correct themselves once they noticed the pattern.

Sliding Keys

Following the sequence

Tracking a moving arrow, reading the blinking keys, and knowing when to shoot competed for attention. Missing one cue made it hard to tell what to do next.

Design priorityTeach the cue before the drill asks athletes to react to it.

02 / First-time clarity

How much should the drill explain up front?

I kept the existing drill and prototyped two ways to teach the same sequence. One explains the rule before play. The other directs attention to the moving cue inside the scene.

The original interaction

The instruction was below the action.

Athletes had to notice the instruction, then find the relevant signal in the scene. A missed blink could leave them guessing at the order.

During the drill

Make progress easy to check.

Some athletes couldn’t tell how long the drill would last. I explored a slim bar, a circular timer, and step bubbles to see which could be read quickly without drawing focus from the targets.

Before the drill

Show athletes what comes next.

I added steps for setup and a short explanation of what each drill trains, with the tutorial still available when athletes want more guidance.

03 / Motivation to return

Give the session results a point of comparison.

The original summary showed how long each drill took. I explored how the same screen could help athletes see change over time. Interviews suggested two possible motivators: improving on their own results and competing with teammates.

Compare with yourself.

Show how the athlete did against their previous session. Comparing the same drill makes the change easier to interpret.

Compare with your team.

Keep team rank separate so it’s available to athletes who find it motivating, without letting it dominate the session recap.

Research limit. Only three of six diary contributors completed the planned entries, and their coach helped prompt participation. That was enough to suggest a follow-up question, but we couldn’t tell what would bring athletes back on their own.

04 / Handoff & reflection

Start by testing whether athletes need less help.

I documented the cue, progress, and summary prototypes in a prioritized test plan. The findings here describe the original experience. We didn’t measure how the redesigns affected independent completion or repeat use.

  1. 01 / Test first

    Can athletes start unaided?

    Give each first-time athlete one cue variant. Track the time to their first correct action, requests for help, and completion without assistance. Then compare the progress displays during play.

  2. 02 / Test next

    What brings athletes back?

    Compare personal progress and team ranking over repeat sessions. Track unprompted returns and use a short check-in to ask what made the next session worthwhile.

What I took forwardI’d pair a usability survey with one concrete task measure: could a new athlete start the drill without someone stepping in?
